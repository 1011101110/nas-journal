## Newborn (Birth – 4 weeks)

**What defines this stage:** NAS scoring, NICU stay or not, and getting through the acute withdrawal period. Most behavioral development is secondary to the medical reality of the first weeks.

---

### What's Happening Neurologically

Newborns exposed to opioids in utero are born dependent. When the opioid supply ends at birth, the nervous system activates. This is withdrawal — not in the pejorative sense, but literally: the nervous system recalibrating without the opioids it organized around.

The Finnegan NAS Scoring System measures withdrawal severity every 3–4 hours: tremors, high-pitched cry, poor feeding, sweating, loose stools, sleep, tone. A score above 8 (or above threshold per the protocol in use) typically triggers pharmacological treatment — usually morphine or methadone taper.

What the score captures is the state of the nervous system under stress. High scores = highly activated nervous system with limited capacity to self-regulate. This is the baseline you're starting from.

---

### The NICU Period

**What to prioritize:**
- Skin-to-skin contact (kangaroo care) when medically allowed — the parent's nervous system directly regulates the infant's. Research on NAS specifically shows skin-to-skin reduces NAS scores.
- Low-stimulation environment: dim lighting, quiet, consistent temperature, minimize unnecessary handling.
- Swaddling: firm, consistent, with hands near face if possible (self-soothing).
- Breastfeeding if possible: associated with better NAS outcomes; buprenorphine in breast milk is a dose that can ease withdrawal.

**What to ask your team:**
- What NAS scoring protocol are you using? (Some hospitals still use Finnegan, some use the newer NICU-specific tools)
- What's the treatment threshold?
- When are we talking about discharge criteria?
- Who is our discharge point of contact for outpatient NAS follow-up?

---

### First Weeks Home

Going home often happens before the nervous system is fully stable. The NICU environment is controlled; home is not.

**Immediate priorities:**

1. **Recreate NICU calm.** Low light, low sound, consistent temperature, consistent routine.
2. **White noise.** Constant, low-frequency. Not music.
3. **Feeding schedule.** Small, frequent feedings. Many infant with POEs feed poorly and need smaller volumes more often. Track intake.
4. **Caregiver sleep.** You cannot co-regulate an infant if you're severely sleep-deprived. Tag rotation is medical necessity, not convenience.

**What to expect:**
- Feeding difficulties are normal and often persist well past newborn phase. ([Feeding](/symptoms/feeding))
- High-pitched inconsolable crying is characteristic. It will decrease, but not immediately. ([Screaming](/symptoms/screaming))
- Sleep is fragmented. NAS newborns have shorter sleep cycles and more light-sleep arousal. ([Sleep](/symptoms/sleep))

---

### What to Track

- **Feeding:** Volume per session, frequency, any vomiting/spit-up
- **Sleep:** Duration of sleep cycles, difficulty settling
- **Behavior:** High-pitched cry episodes, duration, what preceded them, what helped
- **Output:** Wet diapers, stool consistency (loose stools are a NAS withdrawal sign)

This data matters for follow-up appointments and for identifying patterns early.

---

### What Comes Next

The newborn phase is acute crisis management. The nervous system is resetting. Your job is to reduce sensory load, maintain consistent care, and survive.

The patterns established here — responses to stress, caregiver sensitivity, environmental consistency — matter for what comes next. But perfection is impossible. Consistency is enough.
