## Older Toddler (24 – 36 months)

**What defines this stage:** Behavioral patterns consolidate. Language is functional. The preschool transition looms. This is when the work of the previous two years shows its returns — and where gaps are most visible.

---

### What's Happening Neurologically

The 24–36 month window is a critical regulatory maturation period. Executive function — planning, inhibitory control, working memory — is developing rapidly. For NAS kids, this development is present but delayed relative to peers.

The regulatory capacity difference that was hard to distinguish from "typical toddler behavior" at 18 months is now more visible against peers. NAS kids at 3 years may be functioning behaviorally at a 18–24 month level in regulation terms. This is important for:
- Setting realistic expectations
- Communicating with preschool staff
- Advocating for appropriate support

This is not a permanent gap. Regulatory maturation continues into school age and beyond. But it's a real developmental lag that needs to be named.

---

### What to Expect

**Tantrums should be decreasing** if intervention has been consistent. Not absent — decreasing. Frequency, intensity, and recovery time should all show improvement trend by 30–36 months with PCIT + routine. If they're not, reassess the intervention consistency and consider additional support. ([Tantrums](/symptoms/tantrums))

**Preschool readiness is a real challenge.** Preschool introduces exactly the conditions that dysregulate NAS kids: novel environment, group expectations, multiple transitions, sensory complexity, teacher who doesn't know your child's regulation patterns. Preschool may be harder than home — this is expected, not a failure.

**Peer social development.** Parallel play transitions to interactive play. NAS kids often have social-emotional lags — difficulty reading social cues, intense reactions to peer conflict, sensory issues with proximity and noise in group settings.

**Language as a regulatory tool.** By 36 months, many NAS kids can begin to use language in the regulatory sequence: identifying the feeling, communicating distress before it escalates. This is a major developmental milestone. Work toward it explicitly.

---

### Preschool Preparation

**What to communicate to preschool staff:**
- Your child has a lower sensory threshold than peers — describe specifics
- Transition warnings (5-minute and 2-minute) are not optional — they're functional
- Your child's escalation signs and early intervention strategies
- Recovery protocol: what helps, what doesn't
- Not a behavioral problem — a neurological difference requiring accommodation

**What to put in writing:**
- A brief behavior support document, given to the teacher on day 1
- Specific strategies: what triggers, what helps, what to avoid
- Your contact information for quick consultation if needed

**IEP or 504 eligibility:**
- At 3 years, school-based evaluation becomes available
- NAS with documented developmental impact may qualify for IEP services
- If your child has been receiving early intervention, the transition at 3 requires an evaluation for school-based services
- Request this in writing, 6 weeks before the third birthday

---

### Assessing Progress

At 36 months, take stock:
- Is meltdown frequency trending down?
- Is recovery time shortening?
- Can the child communicate distress in words before escalating?
- Is sleep consistent?
- Is feeding expanding?

These are the markers. Imperfect answers to all of them is expected and okay. Flat or worsening trajectory in any area is a signal to reassess the intervention stack.

---

### What's Ahead

The regulatory work doesn't end at 3. But the preschool and early school years are where the investment in the toddler years pays off. Kids with consistent early intervention — PCIT, routine, co-regulation — show meaningfully better behavioral outcomes at 5–7 than kids without it.

The research on NAS long-term outcomes ([Long-Term Outcomes](/research/long-term-outcomes)) is complicated — many variables, many confounds. But the factor that most consistently predicts better outcomes is caregiver stability and environmental consistency. That's within your control.
