## Navigating the Medical System with a POE Child

**The short version:** Children with prenatal opioid exposure (POE) attract more medical attention than typical children — some of it useful, some of it based on assumptions rather than clinical evidence. Navigating this well requires understanding the scrutiny dynamic, using specific clinical language, knowing your rights, and being honest about the fact that outcomes depend partly on social capital you may or may not have.

---

## The Scrutiny Dynamic

Children with prenatal opioid exposure — especially those born into circumstances involving child protective services, foster care, or documented maternal substance use — attract elevated medical scrutiny. More attention, more testing, more assumptions. Some of this is appropriate. Much of it is pattern-matching rather than clinical reasoning applied to your specific child.

Understanding this going in changes how you prepare for appointments.

**This is not hypothetical.** Here is a representative example of a pattern many POE families encounter, drawn from real experience:

Shortly after discharge from the NICU, a well-child visit flagged a small red spot in the eye — a subconjunctival hemorrhage, common in newborns after the physical stress of birth and the weeks of agitation in NAS withdrawal. The clinician reported the finding to the hospital's risk/protective services team, noting explicitly that this was being done "in the name of equity." There was no other clinical basis — no other findings, no pattern of injury, no developmental concern. The referral was made because of the child's documented history.

The protective services team escalated. They requested a full abuse workup: blood draw, skeletal X-ray series, MRI. The foster parents felt they had no choice but to comply. The blood draw was performed on an infant with a hyperactivated stress response — physically clenched, hypersensitive, screaming. The MRI attempt failed; the child couldn't be adequately sedated and the scan was abandoned. Eventually, after significant distress and intervention by the biological mother who understood what was happening, the workup was stood down without findings.

Nothing was found because there was nothing to find. The investigation was initiated based on the child's background, not clinical evidence.

**This pattern is not exceptional.** It is the predictable result of a medical system where POE children's histories function as standing alerts.

### What to Know About Scrutiny

**Red flags that trigger mandatory reporting — and why POE kids generate more of them:**
- Any unexplained bruising, marks, or eye findings in young children
- Developmental delays (which POE kids often present with)
- History of protective services involvement
- Documented maternal substance use in the medical record
- Foster care status

These triggers are structural. Being prepared for them is not paranoid — it's realistic.

**What you can ask for and push back on:**
- Ask for the specific clinical basis for any recommended test or intervention
- Request that concerns be documented in writing before you agree to any workup
- Ask whether the recommendation would be made for a child without POE history
- You can request a second opinion. You can request a patient advocate.

**What you can decline:**
- Non-emergency medical procedures are not compelled. You can decline. Document your declination in writing.
- "Compliance under duress" is not the same as informed consent. If you feel coerced, say so — in the room and in writing afterward.

**Document everything:**
- The date, provider name, what was said, what was recommended, what you agreed to
- Any time you felt pressure you couldn't meaningfully refuse
- Follow up every significant appointment with a written summary email to the provider

## Social Capital in Medical Advocacy

Let's be direct about something the parenting literature usually avoids: advocacy outcomes in medicine are not evenly distributed.

This site's author is a professional with an advanced vocabulary, confidence in clinical settings, and an affect that reads as credible to medical providers. When he says "I'd like to understand the clinical basis for that recommendation before we proceed," it lands differently than the same words from a parent who is already coded as suspect by the system.

This is real. It is unfair. It is not the whole picture, but it is part of the picture.

**For parents who don't start with that same social position:**

- **Use specific clinical language** — the scripts below are here for this. Clinical vocabulary shifts the dynamic.
- **Bring a second adult** — a friend, a partner, an advocate, anyone. A second adult changes how providers interact with you. You are no longer alone in the room.
- **Request things in writing, respond in writing** — email creates a paper trail that verbal conversation doesn't. After any significant appointment, send a brief email summarizing what was discussed and what you agreed to.
- **Use patient advocates** — they exist at every hospital and they are free. Ask for one. They are specifically there to help you navigate the system.
- **Know that you can decline** — document your declination, and escalate to a supervisor if needed. You are not obligated to consent to non-emergency procedures.
- **Keep a written log** — date, provider, what was said, what was recommended, what you agreed to. This matters if a dispute arises later.

The goal is not to fight the system at every turn. The goal is to be an informed participant rather than a passive one.

## Advocating for Early Intervention

The default response from many providers to POE-related developmental concerns is "let's wait and see." For children with prenatal opioid exposure, this is almost always the wrong answer.

The evidence on early intervention is consistent: earlier is better, developmental windows close, and the regulatory capacity built in the first three years has outsized impact on long-term outcomes. Waiting is not neutral.

**What to say when providers suggest waiting:**

- *"I'm aware of the developmental implications of prenatal opioid exposure. I'd like a referral to early intervention services now."*
- *"What is the specific clinical reason to wait rather than refer?"*
- *"I'd like this documented in the visit notes."*

That third one matters. Providers are more careful when they know their reasoning is being documented.

**What to request:**

- **Early intervention evaluation** — this is Part C of IDEA, the federal early intervention entitlement for children 0–3. It is free regardless of insurance, and every state must provide it. Your pediatrician doesn't need to refer you — you can self-refer by contacting your state's early intervention program directly.
- **Physical therapy referral** — if any physical tightness, elevated muscle tone, or movement pattern concerns are present
- **Occupational therapy referral** — for sensory concerns, feeding difficulties, or motor skill delays
- **Speech therapy evaluation** — by 18 months if any communication concerns; earlier if warranted
- **Developmental pediatrician referral** — worth requesting early, given the waitlists (often 6–12 months)

**Expect delays even after referral.** The system is slow. The referral does not mean services start next week. Push anyway. The earlier you are in the queue, the earlier services begin.

## What to Raise at Every Appointment

Providers may not ask about these things. Raise them yourself.

- **Sleep patterns and quantity** — POE kids often sleep more than typical; shifts in pattern are clinically significant
- **Sensory profile** — is the child seeking or avoiding? Both have different intervention implications
- **Transition difficulty** — moving between activities, places, people
- **Feeding issues** — texture aversions, volume, mealtime regulation
- **Muscle tone / physical tightness** — present at birth, may persist
- **Emotional regulation baseline** — how often, how intense, how long to recover
- **Early intervention progress** — is the child receiving services? Are they making progress?

The more specific you are about observations, the more useful the appointment.

## Medicaid and Insurance Navigation

**Part C Early Intervention is federally mandated and free.** It does not matter what insurance your child has or doesn't have. Contact your state's early intervention program directly. Self-referral is available in every state.

**Medicaid covers most therapies:** PT, OT, speech therapy, behavioral health. If you hit resistance, ask for the specific billing codes and the documentation required for authorization.

**When a service is denied:**
1. Request the denial in writing — verbal denials are not official
2. File an appeal — the denial letter should include appeal instructions and deadlines
3. If the appeal fails: contact your state insurance commissioner's office. They have enforcement authority.
4. Document every authorization and every denial

**Private insurance:**
- Coverage for developmental therapies varies. Check your plan's benefit summary for "habilitative services" (different from rehabilitative services and often more broadly covered for developmental conditions)
- Prior authorization requirements are common — understand what's needed before scheduling
- If coverage is denied, the appeal process applies here too

## Finding POE-Aware Providers

Not all pediatricians have experience with prenatal opioid exposure. Not all developmental pediatricians do either.

**Ask directly:** *"Do you have experience with children who have prenatal opioid exposure?"* The answer tells you a lot.

**What to look for:**
- Providers affiliated with developmental pediatrics programs at children's hospitals
- POE/NAS clinics — major children's hospitals in cities with high opioid prevalence often have them. They are worth the travel for an initial evaluation even if you don't use them for ongoing care.
- Providers who have worked with foster care or early intervention systems — they've seen the population
- Early intervention coordinators are often the best referral source for POE-aware therapists. They know who in the community understands this population.

**What to avoid:**
- Providers who respond to POE concerns with "all kids have tantrums" or "he'll grow out of it"
- Providers who can't explain the specific neurobiological mechanism behind their recommendation

## Language That Helps

Clinical language changes outcomes. These scripts are here to use.

**General framing:**
- *"My child has a history of prenatal opioid exposure. I'm concerned about [specific observation]."*
- *"I'd like a referral to [specific service]. What's the process?"*
- *"Can you document that recommendation in the visit notes?"*

**When you want to slow down:**
- *"I'd like to understand the clinical basis for that recommendation before we proceed."*
- *"Is this recommendation standard for children without POE history? If not, what's the clinical distinction?"*

**When you need to decline:**
- *"I'm declining that procedure. Can we discuss alternatives?"*
- *"I want to document this conversation in writing. Can you give me your email?"*

**When advocating for early intervention:**
- *"I'm aware that the developmental implications of prenatal opioid exposure include [specific area]. I'd like to address this proactively."*
- *"What is the specific clinical reason to wait rather than refer to early intervention now?"*

You know your child. You have observed them daily. That observation is clinically relevant data. Name it as such.
