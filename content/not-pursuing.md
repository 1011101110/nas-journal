## What We're Not Pursuing

Given everything on the plate — PCIT practice, routine maintenance, daily co-regulation work, OT, feeding therapy — there's limited capacity for additional interventions. These are things we've looked at and decided not to prioritize, with reasoning.

Tone: This isn't dismissive. Some of these have real advocates. The question is always: given finite time, energy, and money, does the evidence justify the effort?

---

### Food Dyes & Artificial Colors

**What proponents claim:** Artificial food dyes (Red 40, Yellow 5, Yellow 6) cause hyperactivity and behavioral dysregulation. Removing them improves behavior.

**What the evidence actually shows:** The Southampton study (2007) showed a small effect of a mixed dye + sodium benzoate blend on hyperactivity in a general population sample. The effect size was small, the mixture tested isn't commercially available, and replication has been inconsistent. EFSA and FDA have reviewed the evidence and not found sufficient basis for regulatory action. The effect, if it exists, is smaller than the behavioral impact of sleep disruption or inconsistent routine.

**Why it's not a current priority:** The removal effort is significant (food dyes are in most processed foods toddlers accept). The expected effect size is small-to-uncertain. There are higher-ROI interventions in the same time and attention. If the diet is already relatively whole-foods, the exposure is already low.

### Gluten-Free / Casein-Free Diet

**What proponents claim:** Removing gluten (wheat) and casein (dairy) reduces autism spectrum and behavioral symptoms by eliminating "opioid peptides" (gluteomorphins, casomorphins) that allegedly affect brain function.

**What the evidence actually shows:** The opioid peptide hypothesis has not been substantiated in controlled research. Multiple systematic reviews have found no reliable evidence that GFCF diets improve behavioral outcomes in children without confirmed celiac disease or dairy allergy. The studies that show effects are methodologically weak (unblinded, small, no control groups).

**Why it's not a current priority:** GFCF diet is one of the most effort-intensive dietary interventions for families with young children. Dairy and wheat are primary calorie and protein sources for most toddlers, especially selective eaters (which describes most NAS kids). The dietary restriction risk (inadequate calcium, vitamin D, caloric intake) is real. The evidence doesn't justify that risk and effort.

*Exception: if your child has confirmed celiac disease, lactose intolerance, or dairy allergy, elimination is appropriate on clinical grounds, not behavioral ones.*

### Heavy Metal Chelation

**What proponents claim:** Heavy metal accumulation (mercury, lead, arsenic) impairs neurological function. Chelation (medications that bind and remove heavy metals) treats behavioral and developmental issues.

**What the evidence actually shows:** Chelation has evidence for acute heavy metal poisoning. It has no reliable evidence for treating neurodevelopmental disorders in the absence of confirmed heavy metal toxicity. A 2018 Cochrane review found no evidence of benefit for autism-spectrum conditions, and documented real risks: kidney damage, electrolyte imbalances, and in rare cases, death from hypocalcemia.

**Why it's not a current priority:** No confirmed heavy metal toxicity. Meaningful risk profile. No credible evidence of behavioral benefit. This is not in the stack.

---

### Specific Elimination Diets (Various)

**What proponents claim:** Removing specific foods (nightshades, histamine-containing foods, high-oxalate foods, sugar) reduces behavioral symptoms.

**What the evidence actually shows:** For nightshade elimination and oxalate restriction specifically, the evidence base in behavioral pediatrics is essentially anecdotal. The histamine connection has some biological plausibility and limited research in a subset of children with suspected histamine intolerance — but "suspected" is doing heavy lifting. Sugar causing behavioral dysregulation (outside of blood sugar swings from inconsistent meals) is a persistent parenting myth not supported by controlled research.

**Why it's not a current priority:** Young selective eaters (NAS kids in particular) cannot sustain aggressive dietary restriction without significant nutritional risk. The expected benefit is theoretical; the cost to mealtime peace and dietary variety is concrete. Consistent mealtimes and the [Division of Responsibility](/interventions/routines-predictability) approach will do more for mealtime behavior than elimination diets.

### A Note on Future Reconsideration

These decisions are time-and-evidence-relative. If new robust research emerges, or if circumstances change (older child with more dietary flexibility, confirmed test results that change the clinical picture), the calculus may shift. Ruling something out now is not ruling it out permanently.
