## In plain English

Screaming is often the behavior everyone wants to stop first. But the better question is: what is the scream doing?

It may signal pain, hunger, sensory overload, fear, fatigue, transition stress, frustration, or a nervous system that cannot downshift.

## How it changes by age

### 0–6 months {#0-6-months}

Screaming often requires a body-first check: hunger, reflux, constipation, temperature, startle, fatigue, or withdrawal-related sensitivity. Independent calming is not the goal; safe co-regulation is.

### 6–12 months {#6-12-months}

Screaming may connect to separation, teething, feeding changes, sleep disruption, or sensory overload. Look for patterns around naps, meals, car seats, and transitions.

### 1–3 years {#1-3-years}

Toddlers may scream when language, control, sensory needs, and frustration collide. Use fewer words, hold boundaries calmly, and repair after the nervous system comes back online.

### 3–5 years {#3-5-years}

Screaming may show up around demands, transitions, fear, or after preschool overload. More language does not always mean more regulation capacity.

### School age {#school-age}

Screaming may be less frequent but can reflect anxiety, shame, sensory overload, sleep debt, or accumulated school stress. Focus on triggers, recovery, and skill-building outside the crisis.

## What to check first

- Pain, reflux, constipation, hunger, illness, teething
- Sleep debt
- Sensory overload or sensory seeking
- Transition demands
- Separation or fear
- Too many adult words or too much correction

## What to try first

- Make the situation safe.
- Lower sensory load.
- Use fewer words.
- Offer calm co-regulation.
- Look for body causes.
- Repair afterward.

## If it’s not working

If every response makes the screaming worse, the child may already be past threshold. Shift from teaching to stabilizing: safety, body check, sensory/environment check, then repair.

## Stack interactions

Screaming often improves indirectly when sleep, feeding/GI comfort, sensory supports, routines, and caregiver co-regulation improve.
