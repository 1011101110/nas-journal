## Sensory Overload

**The short version:** NAS disrupts sensory processing at a neurological level. The nervous system gates incoming sensory input less effectively, so routine environments feel louder, brighter, and more chaotic than they are.

---

### Why This Happens in NAS Kids

Sensory processing involves two functions the brain performs simultaneously: amplifying important signals and suppressing irrelevant ones. The second function — sensory gating, or suppression — is where NAS kids have a deficit.

Prenatal opioid exposure disrupts development of the thalamus and sensory cortices, which are responsible for filtering sensory input. The result: sensory information that the brain should be filtering as background noise isn't filtered. Everything comes through louder.

This is not sensitivity in a colloquial sense. It's a structural processing difference. The grocery store isn't slightly more stimulating for these kids — it's genuinely overwhelming in a way that's hard to understand without experiencing it.

Specific sensory channels commonly affected:
- **Auditory:** Background noise, crowded environments, sudden loud sounds
- **Tactile:** Certain textures, clothing tags, unexpected touch
- **Visual:** Bright lights, busy visual environments, fast movement
- **Proprioceptive:** Needs more deep pressure input, or conversely, is overwhelmed by light unexpected touch

Some NAS kids are seekers (need more sensory input to feel regulated) and some are avoiders (sensory input destabilizes). Many alternate between both.

---

### Immediate Response

**⚡ When overload is happening:**

1. **Remove from the environment** where possible. Exit the store. Find a quiet corner. Lower the stimulation source.
2. **Reduce inputs systematically:** dim lights, reduce sound, remove visual clutter from immediate field.
3. **Regulate yourself** — your stress reading (voice, face, movement) is a sensory input. Stay calm. ([Co-Regulation](/interventions/co-regulation))
4. **Offer regulating proprioceptive input:** firm hug, weighted blanket, firm hand on back — *if* the child accepts touch at this moment (some won't).
5. **White noise or music** to mask unpredictable environmental noise.
6. **Wait.** Don't talk them through it. Wait for the nervous system to recover.

---

### Structural Approaches

🏗️ **What to build into daily life:**

**Know your child's sensory profile.** You need a list: what inputs consistently trigger overload, what inputs are regulating. Tactile? Auditory? Keep a 1-week log. The patterns are usually clear.

**Create a sensory safe space.** A corner, a tent, a beanbag — a consistent, low-stimulation place the child can go (or be taken to) when overwhelmed. Consistent place + consistent inputs = regulated space.

**Plan environmental exposure deliberately.** Grocery stores, family gatherings, birthday parties — all high-sensory environments. Time these for when the child is rested and fed. Bring regulating tools: noise-canceling headphones, a comfort item, a snack.

**Build sensory regulation into the day:**
- Heavy work before demanding environments (carrying groceries, pushing a stroller, wall push-ups — proprioceptive input is regulating)
- Outdoor time daily — natural environments have lower sensory complexity than built environments
- Water play — highly regulating for most NAS kids (deep pressure + temperature + controlled sensory input)

**Reduce baseline sensory load at home:**
- Remove tags from clothing preemptively
- Consistent clothing textures (soft, seamless socks, etc.)
- Lower ambient sound at home when possible
- Reduce busy visual patterns in primary rooms

**Occupational therapy referral:** If sensory processing is significantly impacting daily function, OT assessment and sensory integration therapy are worth pursuing. OT can map your child's specific sensory profile and create a targeted sensory diet.

### Interventions That Help Here

🔬 **Long Game:**

- **[Co-Regulation](/interventions/co-regulation)** — Parental regulated state directly reduces sensory reactivity in the moment.
- **[Routines & Predictability](/interventions/routines-predictability)** — Predictable environments have lower sensory novelty; baseline cortisol is lower.
- **[Screen Time Reduction](/interventions/screen-time-reduction)** — High-stimulation screens raise the threshold, making real-world sensory environments feel worse.
- **[Omega-3s](/interventions/omega-3s)** — Reduced neuroinflammation slightly reduces sensory hypersensitivity over time.

---

### What Worked For Us ★

**Noise-canceling headphones for kids** (Puro Sound Labs or Ruckus Kids): For grocery stores, airports, and family events. Not isolation — just reducing the peak inputs. The child can still hear and interact; the headphones cut background din.

**Sensory bin** (oats, rice, kinetic sand, water beads): Controlled, self-directed sensory input that most kids find regulating. A 15-minute sensory bin session before a challenging activity reduces activation level going in.
