## In plain English

Sensory overload happens when the child’s nervous system cannot organize the amount, type, or timing of input coming in. The child may scream, shut down, run, hit, cling, laugh wildly, seek crashing pressure, or seem impossible to reach.

The important nuance: not every sensory problem is fixed by making things quieter. Some children are sensory avoidant and need less input. Others are sensory seeking and need the right kind of input before they can calm.

## What this may look like

- melting down in noisy or busy places
- seeming dysregulated after daycare, family visits, stores, or screens
- covering ears, hiding, freezing, or clinging
- crashing, jumping, spinning, pushing, biting, or seeking deep pressure
- becoming louder and faster when adults say “calm down”
- sleep getting worse after sensory-heavy days

## Why it may happen

Children with prenatal exposure may have a stress-response system that moves into threat or high arousal more quickly. Sensory input can become part of that stress load.

A child who looks “behavioral” may actually be overloaded, under-supported, or seeking organizing input.

## How it changes by age

### 0–6 months {#0-6-months}

Look for startle, feeding disruption, difficulty settling, sensitivity to light/noise/touch, and contact needs. Intervention is mostly environment and caregiver co-regulation.

### 6–12 months {#6-12-months}

Mobility adds new sensory input. The child may seek movement but still become overwhelmed. Watch transitions between active play and sleep.

### 1–3 years {#1-3-years}

Toddlers may show sensory needs through climbing, crashing, screaming, running away, tantrums, or resistance to dressing/bathing. Heavy work and predictable routines matter.

### 3–5 years {#3-5-years}

Preschool environments can exceed the child’s sensory capacity. Problems may show up after school rather than during school.

### School age {#school-age}

Sensory overload may look like anxiety, irritability, avoidance, attention problems, or after-school collapse.

## What to try first

- Reduce unpredictable input: noise, bright lights, chaotic transitions.
- Add organizing input: heavy work, deep pressure, pushing/pulling, outdoor movement.
- Use fewer words during overload.
- Build decompression after high-demand environments.
- Track whether the child is seeking input or avoiding it.

## If it’s not working

If sensory strategies backfire, ask:

- Are we treating a sensory seeker like a sensory avoider?
- Are we removing stimulation when the child actually needs organizing input?
- Is the environment too unpredictable?
- Is the child hungry, tired, constipated, or in pain?
- Is the strategy happening too late, after the child is already past threshold?

## What to stack around it

Sensory support often needs sleep, environment, movement, caregiver co-regulation, and OT guidance around it. Sensory strategies work best when they are built into the day, not only used during crisis.

## Clinician / researcher note

Sensory symptoms are a strong example of why POE care should not be siloed. Sensory processing, sleep, GI comfort, movement, and caregiver co-regulation can all change the child’s threshold for overload.
