## Sleep Disruption

**The short version:** POE kids sleep differently because their nervous systems regulate differently. Falling asleep requires a nervous system transition to calm — and that transition is structurally harder for these kids.

---

### Why This Happens in NAS Kids

Sleep is not a passive state — it requires active neurobiological transitions and maintenance. POE kids have disrupted sleep architecture at multiple levels.

**Altered sleep architecture: REM/NREM cycling.** Neonatal sleep consists primarily of active sleep (REM-equivalent) and quiet sleep (NREM-equivalent), cycling approximately every 50–60 minutes. Opioid receptor activation is involved in sleep state regulation — mu-opioid receptors are expressed in sleep-regulating circuits including the ventrolateral preoptic area (VLPO, the sleep "switch") and the locus coeruleus (the waking "switch"). Prenatal opioid exposure disrupts this balance. POE infants show altered sleep architecture on polysomnography: less quiet sleep, more fragmented sleep cycling, and altered REM periods. This fragmentation means POE infants cycle through light sleep stages more frequently, each transition representing an opportunity for arousal.

**Adenosine and sleep pressure.** Adenosine is the brain's primary sleep-promoting molecule — it accumulates during waking hours, building "sleep pressure," and is cleared during sleep (this is why caffeine causes wakefulness: it blocks adenosine receptors). The relationship between the opioid system and adenosine is bidirectional: opioids modulate adenosine signaling, and adenosine is a partial mediator of opioid-induced sleep effects. Prenatal opioid exposure may alter adenosine receptor sensitivity, disrupting the normal sleep pressure accumulation cycle. This is one mechanism by which POE kids sometimes seem overtired but still can't fall asleep — the sleep pressure signal may not be accurately converting to sleep onset.

**The cortisol morning surge and early waking.** The cortisol awakening response (CAR) is a sharp cortisol spike in the first 20–30 minutes after waking — the HPA axis's way of mobilizing energy for the day. In healthy adults and children, the CAR is a modest, organized spike. In POE kids with chronically dysregulated HPA function, the CAR can be exaggerated and disorganized — potentially contributing to the stressed, dysregulated quality of early mornings. Furthermore, early morning light exposure — which POE kids may be sensitive to given their hyperacusis — activates the suprachiasmatic nucleus (the circadian pacemaker) and triggers the cortisol surge. Early waking in POE toddlers is not just inconvenient; it starts the HPA axis at high intensity before the child has adequate parasympathetic buffering from a full night's rest.

**Low vagal tone.** Vagal tone — the measure of parasympathetic nervous system activity, reflected in heart rate variability — is chronically lower in POE kids. Higher vagal tone enables rapid transitions between autonomic states: from active to resting, from alert to drowsy, from drowsy to asleep. Low vagal tone means these transitions are slow and require more external scaffolding. This is the biological basis for why bedtime co-regulation (a regulated caregiver providing calm cues via prosodic voice and calm touch) is so effective for POE kids — it provides the vagal activation the child's own system cannot generate.

**The white noise mechanism.** White noise (and its lower-frequency variant, "brown noise") works by two complementary mechanisms. First, masking: consistent background noise covers unpredictable environmental sounds (door closing, car outside) that would otherwise trigger the orienting response and arouse a light sleeper. Second, stochastic resonance: the addition of a low-level random noise signal to a sensory system can paradoxically improve its signal-to-noise ratio, regularizing sensory processing. For a nervous system with disrupted sensory gating, consistent background noise reduces the "gain" on unexpected inputs. This is not merely soothing — it is mechanistically addressing the sensory processing deficit.

### Immediate Response

**⚡ For bedtime difficulty (child won't settle):**

1. Dim lights in the home 45–60 minutes before bedtime. Bright overhead light suppresses melatonin.
2. Reduce stimulation: quiet activities, calm voices, lower energy play.
3. Warm bath if tolerated — body temperature drop after a warm bath is a sleep onset signal.
4. Consistent pre-sleep sequence: same 3–5 steps, every night. (See: [Routines & Predictability](/interventions/routines-predictability))
5. White noise machine in the bedroom — consistent low-frequency sound masks environmental noise.
6. Parent co-regulation at bedtime: your calm body and voice during the transition is the most powerful sleep support available.

**⚡ For night waking:**
- Brief check-in: calm, low stimulation, minimal talking
- Avoid full room light (kills melatonin, fully wakes the child)
- Brief physical reassurance then return to bed
- Consistency of response matters more than which specific response you choose

### Structural Approaches

🏗️ **What to build into daily life:**

**Consistent bedtime and wake time.** Non-negotiable. The circadian rhythm organizes around these anchors. Irregular bedtimes extend sleep problems indefinitely.

**The bedtime sequence.** 3–5 steps, same order, every night. Not flexible. The sequence cues the nervous system that sleep is coming. After 2–3 weeks, you'll see the child's body relax when the sequence starts.

**Adequate daytime physical activity.** Physical activity increases sleep drive. POE kids who are physically active during the day sleep better. Outdoor time specifically has additional benefits via light exposure (circadian regulation) and proprioceptive input (regulating).

**Limit stimulation in the 60 minutes before bed.** Screens, fast-paced play, novel exciting experiences — all spike cortisol and delay sleep onset. The wind-down window matters.

**Room environment:**
- Darkness — blackout curtains where possible
- White noise — consistent, not music (lyrical content is cognitively activating)
- Cool temperature — sleep is easier in a cool room
- Consistent smell (same bedding, no novel scents) — olfactory consistency is regulating

**Melatonin:** Low-dose melatonin (0.5mg) has evidence for improving sleep onset in kids with neurodevelopmental conditions. Discuss with your pediatrician. It's a sleep-onset aid, not a sedative — it works best given 30–60 minutes before desired sleep time.

### What Worked For Us ★

**White noise machine** (Marpac Dohm or similar analog machine): More effective than app-based white noise because the sound is consistent and doesn't vary algorithmically. Placed near the door to mask hallway sounds.

**Blackout curtains:** Early morning light is the primary cause of early morning waking in toddlers. True blackout (not "room darkening") makes a real difference.
