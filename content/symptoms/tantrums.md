## In plain English

Meltdowns are not always ordinary defiance. For children with prenatal exposure, a meltdown may mean the nervous system has exceeded capacity.

That does not mean boundaries disappear. It means regulation has to come before teaching.

## What this may look like

- screaming, hitting, throwing, collapsing, running away
- getting worse when adults explain or correct
- meltdowns around transitions, hunger, fatigue, sensory overload, or separation
- long recovery time
- shame or clinginess afterward

## How it changes by age

### 0–6 months {#0-6-months}

This is not a tantrum stage. Crying or screaming is communication about body state, sensory load, or need for co-regulation.

### 6–12 months {#6-12-months}

Frustration can rise as mobility and wants increase, but regulation still depends heavily on adults. Focus on safety, rhythm, and reducing overload.

### 1–3 years {#1-3-years}

This is the peak stage for meltdowns. Prenatal exposure may make intensity, duration, and recovery harder. Teach skills after the child is calm, not during the peak.

### 3–5 years {#3-5-years}

Meltdowns may become more tied to transitions, preschool demands, fear, sensory overload, or shame. Collaborative routines and repair become more useful.

### School age {#school-age}

Meltdowns may look like aggression, refusal, shutdown, or avoidance. Consider anxiety, ADHD-like load, learning stress, sleep, and sensory environment.

## What to try first

- Reduce words during escalation.
- Make the environment safer and simpler.
- Use calm, predictable body language.
- Hold the boundary with fewer demands.
- Repair afterward.
- Track the pattern: sleep, hunger, transition, sensory load, time of day.

## If it’s not working

If a behavior plan makes things worse, ask:

- Is the child regulated enough to learn right now?
- Is the plan asking for compliance before safety?
- Is sensory overload or sleep debt driving the behavior?
- Are transitions too abrupt?
- Does the child need co-regulation before consequences or teaching?

## Stack interactions

Meltdown support depends on environment, co-regulation, sleep, sensory input, nutrition/GI, and therapy alignment. A behavior strategy without the surrounding stack may look like it fails, when the child simply cannot access it yet.

## Clinician / researcher note

Meltdowns are a practical place to bridge behavioral, sensory, developmental, and trauma-informed care. Outcome measures should distinguish compliance from regulation capacity and recovery time.
