## Transition Meltdowns

**The short version:** Transitions are hard for NAS kids because their nervous systems are hypervigilant to uncertainty. Every "what comes next?" is a potential cortisol spike. The fix is predictability, not flexibility.

---

### Why This Happens in NAS Kids

Transitions require the prefrontal cortex to manage a sequence: disengage from current activity, tolerate uncertainty about what comes next, shift to new activity. For a neurotypical child, this is effortful but manageable. For a NAS child, each step has elevated cost.

**The uncertainty problem.** NAS kids' stress response systems are tuned to hypervigilance — a legacy of developing in an environment of neurological stress. Uncertainty about what comes next reads as potential threat. Cortisol spikes before the transition even starts. The child arrives at the transition already activated.

**The disengagement problem.** Stopping a preferred activity requires inhibitory control — a prefrontal cortex function that's underdeveloped in NAS kids. Pulling attention away from something engaging while already elevated is genuinely hard.

**The screen amplifier.** Transitions out of screen time are the hardest. Screens provide an unusually high-stimulation, high-dopamine input. The gap between screen and real-world stimulation is largest, and the cortisol spike is worst. ([Screen Time Reduction](/interventions/screen-time-reduction))

---

### Immediate Response

**⚡ When a transition is coming:**

1. **Give the 5-minute warning.** Verbal + visual timer if possible. "In 5 minutes, we're going to put on shoes."
2. **Give the 2-minute warning.** Specific, calm, no negotiating tone.
3. **Name the sequence.** "First shoes, then car, then grandma's house." The brain needs to know what happens next.
4. **Follow through calmly.** If you said 2 minutes, 2 minutes. Consistency is the regulator.

**⚡ When the transition meltdown is happening:**
- Don't negotiate on the transition itself (back to the screen, back to the toy). You'll extend the meltdown and teach that escalation works.
- Acknowledge the feeling: "I know it's hard to stop. You're upset."
- Stay regulated. Your calm voice and body is the primary tool. ([Co-Regulation](/interventions/co-regulation))
- Get through the transition, then offer reconnection: "That was hard. You did it. Let's _____."

---

### Structural Approaches

🏗️ **What to build into daily life:**

**Transition ritual.** Build a consistent 3-step transition sequence: warning → acknowledge → move. Every time. This sequence itself becomes predictable and regulating.

**Stable daily rhythm.** When the child knows that lunch always comes after park time, the transition from park isn't uncertain. Predictable sequences reduce per-transition cortisol cost. ([Routines & Predictability](/interventions/routines-predictability))

**Visual schedules.** A picture-based daily schedule on the wall lets the child see the sequence. "See, park is done, then lunch, then rest." The schedule is the authority — not you — which reduces the relational tension of you being the one to stop the fun.

**Reduce high-stimulation activities before hard transitions.** If you need to get out the door, don't start a screen session 10 minutes before. Front-load physical activity or calmer play before a demanding transition.

**Practice easy transitions.** Intentionally create low-stakes "transition practice" in your day. "Let's move from the kitchen to the living room" — give warnings, follow through, celebrate. Build the neural pathway for successful transitions before the hard ones.

### Interventions That Help Here

🔬 **Long Game:**

- **[Routines & Predictability](/interventions/routines-predictability)** — Makes most daily transitions predictable sequences rather than uncertainty events.
- **[Co-Regulation](/interventions/co-regulation)** — Your regulated presence is the most immediate lever during active transition meltdowns.
- **[PCIT](/interventions/pcit)** — Builds underlying regulation capacity; makes the child's nervous system more resilient to transition stress.
- **[Screen Time Reduction](/interventions/screen-time-reduction)** — Reduces the hardest transitions (screen-off) and lowers baseline stimulation threshold.

### What Worked For Us ★

The Time Timer visual countdown clock is irreplaceable for transition management. When the child can see time disappearing rather than just hearing "5 more minutes," the transition warning becomes real and concrete.
