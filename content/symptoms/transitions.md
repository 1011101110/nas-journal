## In plain English

Transitions are hard because they require the child to stop one state, predict another, shift attention, tolerate loss of control, and regulate the body during the change.

For children with prenatal exposure, that shift may be much harder than it looks.

## How it changes by age

### 0–6 months {#0-6-months}

Transitions are mostly state changes: feeding to sleep, arms to crib, awake to drowsy, car seat to home. Keep handling slow and predictable.

### 6–12 months {#6-12-months}

Separation and mobility make transitions more emotional. Use repeated cues, familiar objects, and enough time for the child’s body to catch up.

### 1–3 years {#1-3-years}

Toddlers need predictability and some control. Warnings, visual cues, transition objects, and small jobs can reduce panic and power struggles.

### 3–5 years {#3-5-years}

Preschoolers can understand more, but still may not regulate through abrupt changes. Use concrete plans, practice routines, and decompression after school/daycare.

### School age {#school-age}

Transitions may involve executive function, anxiety, sensory shifts, and school demands. Written routines, timers, and collaborative planning may help.

## What this may look like

- meltdowns leaving the house, car, bath, playground, or daycare
- panic when routines change
- refusing even preferred activities if the transition is abrupt
- worse transitions when hungry, tired, overstimulated, or rushed

## What to try first

- Preview what is coming.
- Use the same transition phrase.
- Give visual or concrete cues.
- Reduce hurry where possible.
- Offer a job: carry, push, choose, help.
- Add co-regulation before the demand.

## If it’s not working

Ask:

- Is the child being asked to transition while already overloaded?
- Is the next step predictable enough?
- Is there a sensory barrier: clothing, car seat, noise, light, temperature?
- Is hunger, fatigue, or GI discomfort lowering tolerance?
- Are adults adding too many words?

## Stack interactions

Transitions improve when the environment is predictable, the child has enough sleep and food, sensory needs are anticipated, and adults provide calm co-regulation before the child is past threshold.
