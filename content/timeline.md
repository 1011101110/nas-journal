## Our Journey

A chronological record. Not a story — an account. What happened, what we tried, what worked, what didn't, and what we learned. Written in real time as the situation continues to evolve.

The child is currently 2.5 years old. The "We Are Here" marker below shows where we are now.

---

## NICU: 0 – 8 Weeks

**Context:** Born full-term in Philadelphia. Admitted to the NICU for neonatal abstinence syndrome (NAS) — the acute opioid withdrawal syndrome that follows prenatal opioid exposure.

**What the NICU period looked like:**

The NICU stay extended longer than typical for NAS infants. By 8 weeks, he had nearly outgrown the bassinet — not because of his size (he was at the 2nd percentile for both height and weight at birth), but because of the sheer physical intensity of his presence. Head circumference was above average — a detail the team flagged and we've tracked since.

Nursing staff described him as one of the loudest babies they'd ever cared for. This was not hyperbole. That loudness has persisted.

**Foster care placement:** At 8 weeks, while still in the NICU, he was placed in foster care. We became his foster parents while he was still on monitors.

**The xylazine question:** He was born during a period when Philadelphia's drug supply was heavily contaminated with xylazine (tranq) — an alpha-2 adrenergic agonist used in veterinary medicine, not FDA approved for humans, and not reversed by naloxone. By 2022–2023, a substantial proportion of street fentanyl in Philadelphia contained xylazine.

Xylazine has a completely different pharmacological mechanism than opioids. What does prenatal exposure to an alpha-2 adrenergic agonist do to a developing nervous system? We don't fully know. The existing POE research baseline assumes opioid exposure. His presentation may reflect exposures that fall outside that baseline. We've written a [dedicated research page](/research/xylazine-polysubstance) on this.

**Key facts at discharge from NICU:**
- 2nd percentile height and weight
- Above-average head circumference
- Polysubstance exposure (opioids + probable xylazine)
- Full-term birth, extended NICU stay
- Placed with foster family at 8 weeks

## Home: 8 Weeks – 3 Months

**Context:** First weeks at home. Figuring out the basics with a baby whose nervous system was highly dysregulated.

**What we tried and what we learned:**

*The Mamaroo:* We had it. He rejected it. The rhythmic motion did not calm him — if anything, the mechanical quality seemed to increase his activation. Not every soothing tool works for every POE infant.

*Baths:* Initially highly aversive. He would scream through them. The intervention that changed this: a tip from a therapist to swaddle him in the bath — keep him swaddled with arms contained, and slowly lower him into the water. The reduction in the "arms-free" vulnerability position shifted the whole sensory experience. This worked.

*Sleep position:* He could not sleep on his back. The startle reflex (Moro reflex) was extremely activated — arms flying out, full arousal. We made the decision to allow stomach sleeping, against standard back-to-sleep guidance. This is a risk decision we made with eyes open: we purchased an Owlet sleep sock to monitor oxygen saturation and heart rate continuously, slightly elevated the bassinet head, and used a white noise machine (we settled on the Dohm fan model, which produces genuine fan-generated white noise rather than electronic approximation). Sleep quantity was high — consistently at the upper end of age-appropriate recommendations.

*Sensory profile emerging:* He was a sensory seeker, not a sensory avoider. Drawn toward lights, sounds, stimulation. Not overwhelmed by the world — pulled toward it. Loud. Intense. The nervous system that had been dysregulated by withdrawal was now hunting for input.

## Early Intervention Advocacy: 3 – 6 Months

**Context:** We knew from the beginning that prenatal opioid exposure has well-documented implications for developmental trajectory. We pushed for early intervention immediately.

**What happened:**

We advocated from the first pediatric appointment. Despite that advocacy, scheduling still took months. This is a systemic gap — the referral system is slow, waitlists exist, and "wait and see" is the default for providers who don't know the POE literature. For families without the social capital to push back effectively, delays are longer. We've covered this in the [Medical System guide](/navigating-medical).

First therapies initiated during this window:
- **Physical Therapy (PT):** Focus on motor milestones — head control, muscle tone, movement patterns
- **Occupational Therapy (OT):** Sensory regulation, functional development

**Physical presentation at this stage:** His limbs were consistently clenched. Muscle tone was elevated. His body appeared braced — arms drawn in, legs tense, posture guarded. This is consistent with a nervous system wired in a state of chronic stress. The developmental milestone gap was visible at this stage. With intervention, it began closing.

**What we took from this period:** The advocacy window starts at birth. Waiting until 6 months to push for PT/OT is losing developmental time. Part C of IDEA (the federal early intervention entitlement for ages 0–3) means services are available and free regardless of insurance. The system won't come to you.

## Infant: 6 – 12 Months

**Context:** Therapies underway. Development moving.

**What we saw:**

Sensory seeking continued — lights, noise, stimulation. He was not overstimulated by the world; he demanded more of it. Sleep quantity remained high. The crying was extreme in volume. We started using Loop earplugs and silicone earplugs during the worst episodes to stay regulated ourselves — a caregiver self-protection measure that deserves more discussion than it gets.

Developmentally: catching up with PT/OT support. The milestone gap that was visible at 3 months was closing. Not eliminated, but closing.

The developmental trajectory with consistent early intervention is measurably different from without it. We don't have a counterfactual for this child, but the research is clear and our observation aligned with it.

## Early Toddler: 12 – 18 Months

**Context:** Walking. New phase of dysregulation challenges. Sleep pattern shifts.

**What happened:**

Around 18 months, his sleep pattern shifted. He started waking early — before 5am — and was highly dysregulated by the early waking itself. The dysregulation compounded: he woke dysregulated, which made the day start from a deficit.

**The intervention that worked:** Early morning walks. My wife would be outside at 5am, walking with him. The proprioceptive input, the movement, the sensory experience of being outside — it regulated him. We built a consistent routine around it.

**The challenge:** This worked until winter. When outdoor walks became impractical, the regulation strategy wasn't available. We've since built indoor versions, but the seasonal transition was genuinely difficult.

**Growth:** By 18 months, he was tracking at the 90th percentile for height and weight. From 2nd percentile at birth. This is significant catch-up growth — the body recovering from prenatal nutritional deprivation. It's also a reminder that what looks like one number at one point in time is not a fixed trajectory.

## 📍 We Are Here: 2.5 Years (Current)

**The current moment.**

He is 2.5 years old. He is loud, intense, physically enormous relative to his birth percentile, affectionate, funny, and still hard in ways that require daily deliberate management.

**What is starting now:**

- **Theraplay** — beginning soon. An attachment-focused therapy that works directly on the relationship through structured play organized around structure, engagement, nurture, and challenge. Designed specifically for children with relational and regulatory deficits. [More on Theraplay](/interventions/theraplay)

- **Child-Directed Therapy (CDT)** — beginning soon. Non-directive play therapy where the child leads and the therapist follows, building internal locus of control and self-regulatory capacity. [More on CDT](/interventions/child-directed-therapy)

**The ongoing work:**

We are not at a finish line. The nervous system work continues. The regulatory capacity continues to build. The intervention tracks are cumulative — each one adds.

This site will be updated as learnings from Theraplay and CDT accumulate. We don't know yet what those pages will say.
