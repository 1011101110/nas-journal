## ⚡ Try This Right Now

For an infant who won't eat:
1. **Check hunger cues early** — don't wait until frantic; frantic babies can't eat
2. **Paced bottle feeding** — horizontal bottle, brief pauses, let them control the pace
3. **Burp frequently** — every 1-2 oz, not just at the end
4. **Calm environment** — dim, quiet; feeding is already hard, don't add sensory load
5. **Upright position** — NAS babies often have more reflux

For a toddler who won't eat:
1. **One safe food always present** — something they'll reliably eat
2. **No pressure, no coercion** — division of responsibility: you decide what, child decides how much
3. **Small portions** — a pile of food is overwhelming; tiny amounts, refill as needed
4. **Eat together** — modeling is powerful

## Feeding in the NICU

NAS babies often have dysregulated suck-swallow-breathe reflexes. The suck is disorganized — too fast, too slow, uncoordinated. This is neurological, not behavioral.

NICU feeding strategies:
- **Slow-flow nipple** — gives more time to coordinate
- **Paced feeding** — pause every few sucks to let them breathe and swallow
- **Side-lying position** — can help with coordination
- **Small, frequent feeds** — less volume per feeding, more often

Many NAS families struggle with breastfeeding. Some make it work. Some need to pump and bottle-feed. Some need formula. All of these are valid choices. The goal is nutrition and bonding — the method is secondary.

## Transitioning to Solids

NAS babies may show texture sensitivities that complicate the transition to solids. Common patterns:
- Gagging on anything that isn't smooth
- Strong preferences for specific brands/preparations
- Extreme rejection of new foods

Feeding therapy can help. A feeding therapist will assess oral motor function, sensory responses to food, and the feeding relationship.

**Food chaining** is the gold standard approach: start with accepted foods, make micro-changes in the direction of more variety. Gradual exposure without pressure.
