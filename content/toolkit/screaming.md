## ⚡ Try This Right Now

1. **Check the basics first** — hunger, diaper, temperature (NAS babies sweat more)
2. **Swaddle tight** — arms in, firm but not restrictive
3. **White noise up loud** — match the intensity of the crying
4. **Vertical carry, slight jiggle** — upright position often helps
5. **Skin-to-skin** — your heartbeat and smell are regulating
6. **Dark, quiet room** — reduce all sensory input immediately
7. **Don't panic** — your nervous system is contagious; regulate yourself first

## The NAS Cry Is Different

I need to say this because it took me weeks to understand: the NAS cry is not a manipulation. It is not a bad habit. It is a nervous system that is dysregulated at a neurological level.

Opioids during fetal development affect the way the central nervous system processes stimulation. The result is a baby whose baseline sensitivity is much higher than typical. What would be "a little loud" for most newborns is "overwhelming" for a NAS baby.

The cry itself sounds different. Higher pitched. More urgent. A specific quality that NICU nurses recognize immediately. Once you hear it, you understand why it's so hard to ignore — because it's supposed to be hard to ignore. It's saying: *I am overwhelmed and I need help.*

## What Works, By Age

**Newborn/NICU:** Containment, skin-to-skin, white noise, swaddling. Minimal stimulation. These babies are not being "spoiled" by being held — they are being regulated.

**1-6 months:** Same as above, plus adding movement patterns. Side-lying while holding, gentle jiggle (not shake), slow rhythmic motion. We used a specific bouncing rhythm that worked — about 60 beats per minute, like a slow heartbeat.

**6-18 months:** Begins to respond to voice. Talking through what's happening helps. "I hear you. You're upset. I'm right here." Validation before solutions.

**18 months+:** Language helps. Emotion coaching begins to work. "That was so loud and it scared you." Naming it helps the nervous system process it.

## What Doesn't Help

- **Letting them "cry it out"** in the traditional sense, especially young — dysregulated NAS babies do not naturally calm down; they escalate
- **Adding more stimulation** (turning on lights, adding sounds) — this amplifies the overload
- **Anxious handling** — babies feel tension in the body that holds them
- **Demanding eye contact** during a meltdown — this is stimulating, not calming
