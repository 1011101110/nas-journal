## ⚡ Try This Right Now

During a meltdown:
1. **Stay calm** — your regulation is contagious
2. **Don't escalate** — lower your voice, slow your movements
3. **Say less** — "I'm here" is enough; don't try to reason during peak
4. **Ensure safety** — move dangerous objects, don't restrain unless safety requires it
5. **Wait it out** — NAS meltdowns often need to complete; you can't shortcut
6. **Reconnect after** — once calm, brief reconnection: "That was really hard. I love you."

## Tantrums vs. Meltdowns

These are different, and the distinction matters:

**Tantrum** = frustrated by not getting something they want. Child has some control. Stops when they get what they want or the interaction changes. Purpose: to influence you.

**Meltdown** = nervous system overwhelmed. Child has no control. Doesn't stop when demands are met. No purpose — it's not strategic, it's dysregulation.

NAS kids can have both. Meltdowns are more common because the sensory/emotional threshold is lower. Responding to a meltdown like a tantrum (firm limits, consequences) makes things worse. Responding to a tantrum like a meltdown (pure comforting, no expectations) misses the behavior piece.

Learning to tell them apart is worth the effort.

## The PCIT Framework

Parent-Child Interaction Therapy (PCIT) is an evidence-based approach particularly well-suited to NAS kids. The first phase, Child-Directed Interaction, builds the relationship foundation:

**PRIDE skills:**
- **P**raise specific behavior ("I love how you're stacking those blocks")
- **R**eflect what they say (child: "car!" → parent: "Yes, a red car!")
- **I**mitate their play
- **D**escribe what you see ("You're putting the puzzle piece in")
- **E**nthusiasm — genuine engagement

No questions. No commands. No criticism. Just following their lead.

From this foundation, the second phase (Parent-Directed Interaction) introduces clear, consistent, calm expectations. Because you've built the relationship foundation first, the child is more receptive.

## Choices, Warnings, and Routine

Three tools that reduce meltdown frequency:

**Advance warning:** "Five more minutes, then we're leaving." "Two minutes." "One minute." The transition doesn't surprise the nervous system.

**Limited choices:** "Do you want the red cup or the blue cup?" Choice creates agency. Agency reduces power struggle. Keep choices simple — two options max.

**Predictable routine:** When a NAS kid knows exactly what comes next, their nervous system can relax. The routine IS the regulation.
