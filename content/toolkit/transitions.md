## ⚡ Try This Right Now

For an upcoming transition:
1. **Give warnings** — 10 minutes, 5 minutes, 2 minutes, "it's time"
2. **Narrate what's happening** — "We're leaving the park. We're going to the car. Then we're going home."
3. **Bring something with you** — a comfort object that bridges the change
4. **Acknowledge the loss** — "I know it's hard to leave. The park will be here next time."
5. **Have the next thing be something good** — "When we get home, we'll have a snack"

## Why Transitions Are Hard

Every transition involves a loss — of the current thing — and an uncertainty — about the next thing.

For NAS kids, uncertainty is especially activating. The nervous system that developed in an unpredictable prenatal environment has often calibrated for hypervigilance. The unknown feels threatening even when it isn't.

Additionally, NAS kids often have difficulty with cognitive flexibility — shifting mental set from one thing to another. This is a neurological characteristic, not defiance.

Understanding this changes how you respond. Your child is not refusing to leave the park to upset you. They're experiencing genuine distress at the cognitive-neurological shift required.

## Visual Schedules

Visual schedules are among the most powerful tools for transition difficulty:

**For young toddlers:** Simple picture cards showing the sequence of events. "First park, then car, then home, then snack." The visual representation of the future reduces the uncertainty of the unknown.

**For older toddlers:** A morning routine chart. Pictures in sequence: wake up, bathroom, get dressed, breakfast, shoes, backpack, go. Same every day. When there's a disruption, preview it: "Today is different. Today we go to the doctor first, then school."

**First-Then boards:** A simpler version. "First this, then that." Used when you need to get through something hard: "First shoes, then playground."

## Transition Objects

Bringing something from the current environment to the next one creates a sensory and emotional bridge. A toy from home that comes to the doctor's office. A small stone from the park that rides along in the car.

This isn't a security blanket in the dismissive sense — it's an anchoring object that says "the good things don't disappear when you move."
