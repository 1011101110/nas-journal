# NAS / Prenatal Opioid Exposure Support Site — Proposed UX Layout & Redesign Roadmap

**Working document for redesign**  
**Created:** 2026-04-30  
**Purpose:** Define the information architecture, UX strategy, page templates, design system direction, and implementation roadmap for redesigning the site around parents, clinicians, and researchers.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

The site should not be redesigned as a simple content library or medical explainer. It should become a **translation layer** between three audiences that are often siloed:

1. **Parents and caregivers** who need practical, humane guidance when standard parenting advice is not working.
2. **Clinicians** who need a clearer integrated-care model for children with prenatal opioid exposure / NAS / POE.
3. **Researchers** who need visibility into real-world complexity, intervention gaps, and cross-domain questions.

The core organizing thesis:

> Children with prenatal opioid exposure often do not improve through one intervention alone. Progress comes from stacking supports across environment, caregiver co-regulation, therapy, movement, sleep, nutrition, medical care, and time — then troubleshooting the stack when individual interventions fail.

The redesign should make this thesis visible everywhere.

The site’s differentiator is not merely “information about NAS.” It is:

> A practical and research-informed guide to prenatal opioid exposure, focused on stacking environment, therapy, movement, nutrition, sleep, and caregiver support — and troubleshooting what to do when standard interventions do not work.

## 2. Primary UX Problem

Most parenting, clinical, and educational resources assume interventions work in a relatively linear way:

- establish a routine
- use consistent behavior strategies
- improve sleep hygiene
- add therapy
- adjust diet
- use sensory supports

For prenatal opioid exposure, this is often insufficient.

Parents may do the “right” intervention and still see it collapse because the child’s nervous system is operating at a much higher difficulty level. A sleep routine may fail because of sensory need, GI discomfort, circadian issues, hyperarousal, separation distress, caregiver exhaustion, or reinforcement loops. A behavior plan may fail because the child is not regulated enough to access it. OT may help in-session but not generalize home because the home environment, sleep debt, or nutrition layer is unstable.

The UX must therefore avoid presenting advice as isolated tips.

Instead, the site should answer four questions repeatedly:

1. **What is happening?**
2. **Why might this be harder in prenatal exposure?**
3. **What can we try?**
4. **What do we do when that does not work?**

## 3. Audience Strategy

### 3.1 Parent / Caregiver Audience

Parents need:

- plain-language explanations
- validation without fatalism
- immediate next steps
- troubleshooting pathways
- age-specific guidance
- language to use with clinicians, schools, therapists, and family members
- reassurance that intervention failure is not automatically parent failure

Tone for parents:

- calm
- practical
- compassionate
- non-blaming
- realistic
- not overly clinical at the top level

Parent-facing promise:

> If the standard advice is not working, this site helps you understand what else may be interfering and how to adjust the stack around your child.

### 3.2 Clinician Audience

Clinicians need:

- clinically credible framing
- developmental nuance
- practical cross-domain care models
- awareness that standard interventions may require adaptation
- parent-observed patterns worth taking seriously
- mechanisms and evidence boundaries
- language that bridges medicine, therapy, development, and home life

Clinician-facing promise:

> This site helps clinicians see how POE-related regulation challenges may interact across sleep, sensory processing, feeding/GI, movement, caregiver co-regulation, therapy, and environment.

### 3.3 Researcher Audience

Researchers need:

- clear mechanisms and hypotheses
- gaps between literature and lived experience
- intervention-combination questions
- developmental-stage differences
- real-world failure points
- xylazine-era uncertainty and exposure complexity
- research questions generated by practical care problems

Researcher-facing promise:

> This site identifies where real-world POE care is more complex than single-variable interventions and highlights questions that deserve integrated study.

## 4. Core Content Model

The redesign should organize content through three intersecting lenses:

1. **Age lens** — What changes by developmental stage?
2. **Challenge lens** — What specific issue is the family facing?
3. **Stack lens** — What environmental, therapeutic, movement, nutrition, sleep, relational, and medical supports interact here?

The site should allow users to enter from any of these lenses and move between them.

### 4.1 Age Lens

Age is not a category that lives separately from the rest of the site. Age should act as a filter or lens applied to challenges and interventions.

Suggested age bands:

- Prenatal / newborn discharge context
- 0–6 months
- 6–12 months
- 1–3 years / toddler
- 3–5 years / preschool
- 6–12 years / school age
- Adolescence, if relevant later

Each age band should answer:

- what may be developmentally normal
- what may be intensified by prenatal exposure
- what interventions look like at this age
- what to avoid over-interpreting
- what to track
- when to escalate

### 4.2 Challenge Lens

Challenge pages are likely the highest-traffic practical pages.

Initial challenge categories:

- Sleep
- Early waking
- Self-soothing / settling
- Sensory seeking
- Sensory avoidance
- Meltdowns / shutdowns
- Feeding / GI discomfort
- Constipation / reflux patterns
- Transitions
- Hyperactivity / impulsivity
- Anxiety / vigilance
- Separation distress
- Daycare / school behavior
- Therapy not translating home
- Behavior plans making things worse

Challenge pages should not simply explain the challenge. They should include age-specific guidance, stack interactions, and troubleshooting.

### 4.3 Stack Lens

The stack explains why one intervention may fail alone and why layered supports may produce better results.

Proposed stack layers:

1. **Environment**
   - predictability
   - sensory load
   - routines
   - sleep environment
   - school/daycare setup
   - transition structure

2. **Relationship / Co-regulation**
   - caregiver nervous system as part of intervention
   - safety cues
   - repair after dysregulation
   - realistic expectations around self-soothing
   - reducing shame and threat

3. **Sleep**
   - multiplier for regulation, learning, behavior, and caregiver capacity
   - also often one of the hardest layers to fix

4. **Movement / Exercise / Sensory Input**
   - heavy work
   - proprioception
   - vestibular input
   - outdoor play
   - rhythm
   - exercise as regulation, not punishment

5. **Nutrition / Physiology**
   - blood sugar stability
   - protein
   - GI discomfort
   - constipation/reflux
   - micronutrients
   - careful supplement logic

6. **Therapy / Skill Building**
   - OT
   - speech/feeding therapy
   - trauma-informed therapy
   - behavioral support
   - developmental pediatrics
   - school services

7. **Medical / Research Layer**
   - what is known
   - what is plausible
   - what is unknown
   - xylazine-era uncertainty
   - clinical escalation points
   - research questions

## 6. Homepage Design Specification

The homepage should not feel like a generic resource directory. It should immediately communicate the site’s unique thesis.

### 6.1 Homepage Goals

- Explain who the site is for
- Make parents feel seen without oversimplifying
- Signal clinical/research seriousness
- Introduce the stack model
- Route users quickly by need
- Surface “when it’s not working” as a core promise

### 6.2 Homepage Structure

#### Section 1: Hero

Suggested headline options:

1. **Prenatal exposure is complex. Support has to be layered.**
2. **A practical guide to prenatal opioid exposure, regulation, and what to do when standard advice does not work.**
3. **Understanding prenatal opioid exposure through regulation, development, and stacked support.**

Suggested subhead:

> A parent-built, research-informed guide for families, clinicians, and researchers — focused on stacking environment, therapy, movement, sleep, nutrition, and caregiver support to help children with prenatal exposure thrive.

Primary buttons:

- Start here
- Find help by challenge

Secondary buttons/links:

- Build the stack
- For clinicians & researchers

Design notes:

- Avoid stock medical imagery.
- Use warm but credible visual language.
- The hero should feel calm, not clinical-cold or inspirational-cheesy.
- A simple diagram of stacked supports may be useful above the fold.

#### Section 2: “If the usual advice isn’t working…”

Purpose: validate the core pain.

Copy direction:

> Many children with prenatal exposure need more than a single intervention. Sleep routines, behavior plans, therapy, nutrition changes, and sensory strategies may all help — but they often work best when the surrounding stack is strong enough to support them.

Cards:

- Sleep plans falling apart
- Sensory strategies backfiring
- Therapy not translating home
- Behavior plans making things worse
- Early waking / inability to self-soothe

This can be implemented as:

- segmented control
- jump links
- expandable sections
- page-level filter

Use visible anchor sections with a sticky “Choose age” jump menu. This is simpler, accessible, and SEO-friendly. A richer filter can come later.

### 8.2 Age Section Template

Each age section should include:

- What this may look like at this age
- What is developmentally normal vs potentially intensified
- What to try first
- What not to expect yet
- Common failure points
- What to stack around it
- When to ask for help

### 8.3 Example: Sleep by Age

#### 0–6 Months

Focus:

- feeding/sleep coupling
- reflux/GI discomfort
- contact sleep
- short sleep stretches
- high co-regulation need
- distinguishing realistic sleep expectations from red flags

Avoid overemphasis on independent self-soothing.

#### 6–12 Months

Focus:

- early waking
- frequent night waking
- nap instability
- separation distress
- caregiver proximity
- light exposure
- feeding schedule
- self-soothing limits

Troubleshooting:

- Is the child hungry at 5am?

#### 3–5 Years

Focus:

- fears

#### School Age

Focus:

- attention/ADHD-like patterns

## 9. “When It’s Not Working” Framework

This should be one of the defining features of the site.

### 9.1 Core Principle

> Intervention failure is information. It may mean the child needs a different sequence, a stronger supporting stack, or a different assumption about what is driving the behavior.

### 9.2 Troubleshooting Model

For any intervention that is failing, guide users through:

1. **Is the goal developmentally realistic?**
   - Example: independent self-soothing expectations differ greatly between 6 months and 2 years.

2. **Is the child regulated enough to access the intervention?**
   - A behavior strategy may fail if the child is already beyond threshold.

3. **Is another body system interfering?**
   - sleep debt, hunger, constipation, reflux, pain, allergies, illness, medication effects

4. **Is the sensory profile being misread?**
   - sensory seeking vs sensory avoidance require different responses

5. **Is the environment working against the plan?**
   - light, noise, unpredictability, transitions, school/daycare mismatch

6. **Is the intervention sequence wrong?**
   - maybe movement must come before sleep
   - maybe GI discomfort must be addressed before behavior
   - maybe co-regulation must come before independence

7. **Is the plan too hard for caregiver capacity?**
   - consistency matters, but impossible plans fail

8. **Has the intervention been tested long enough, but not too long?**
   - define a review window
   - avoid changing everything daily
   - avoid persisting for months with no learning

9. **What data should be tracked?**
   - wake time
   - bedtime
   - naps
   - food timing
   - GI symptoms
   - sensory input
   - meltdowns
   - therapy days
   - environmental changes

10. **What is the next smallest adjustment?**
   - change one variable at a time when possible
   - stack support rather than overhaul everything

### 9.3 Troubleshooting Page Pattern

Each “not working” page should include:

- “First, you are not alone” validation
- common reasons this fails in POE/NAS
- age-specific failure points
- stack check
- what to try next
- what to stop doing
- what to track for 1–2 weeks
- questions to ask clinician/therapist
- red flags

## 10. Design System Direction

### 10.1 Visual Tone

The site should feel:

- warm
- grounded
- trustworthy
- evidence-informed
- calm
- practical
- serious without being sterile

Avoid:

- generic baby stock photos
- overly bright pediatric colors
- hospital-like coldness
- wellness influencer aesthetics
- miracle-cure tone
- walls of dense academic text

### 10.2 Color Direction

Recommended palette direction:

- warm off-white / cream background
- deep slate or charcoal text
- muted blue or teal for trust and clinical credibility
- soft green for growth/regulation
- warm amber for caution/attention
- muted rose or clay for human warmth

Functional colors:

- Blue/teal: science, clinician/research notes
- Green: practical supports / what helps
- Amber: troubleshooting / caution
- Red should be used sparingly for urgent red flags only

### 10.3 Typography

Requirements:

- highly readable body text
- strong hierarchy
- comfortable line length
- accessible sizes

Recommendation:

- Body text: 17–18px on desktop, 16px mobile
- Line height: 1.6–1.75 for long-form content
- Max content width: 680–760px for reading sections
- Wider layout only for cards, diagrams, index pages, comparison sections

### 10.4 Components

Core components needed:

#### Audience Layer Cards

Used to separate parent, clinician, and researcher content.

- Parent view
- Clinician note
- Research note

#### Age Lens Selector

Used on challenge pages.

- sticky mini-nav or segmented tabs
- accessible anchor links for initial implementation

#### Stack Diagram

Reusable visual representation of layered intervention.

Variants:

- vertical stack
- radial wheel
- dependency map

Initial recommendation: vertical stack for clarity.

#### Troubleshooting Checklist

Reusable checklist component.

Sections:

- body
- sensory
- environment
- relationship
- schedule
- intervention fit
- caregiver capacity

#### “Try First / Then Consider” Component

Avoids overwhelming parents.

Example:

- Try first
- If still stuck
- Ask about
- Track this

#### Evidence / Uncertainty Badge

Used for interventions.

Possible labels:

- Stronger evidence
- Emerging evidence
- Plausible mechanism
- Parent-observed / research gap
- Use with clinician

This helps credibility and prevents overclaiming.

#### Red Flag Callout

For clinical escalation.

Must be visually distinct but not alarmist.

#### Related Stack Links

At bottom of each page:

- Related age guide
- Related challenge
- Related stack layer
- Related troubleshooting page

## 12. Content Examples

### 12.1 Site Positioning Statement

> This site is for families, clinicians, and researchers trying to understand why children with prenatal exposure may need more layered support than standard advice assumes. It focuses on regulation, development, and the interaction between environment, therapy, movement, sleep, nutrition, caregiver support, and medical care.

### 12.2 Core Thesis Statement

> For prenatal exposure, the question is often not “which intervention works?” but “what conditions does this child need before the intervention can work?”

### 12.3 Troubleshooting Statement

> When a strategy fails, it does not always mean the strategy was wrong or the parent did it badly. It may mean another layer of the stack is overloaded.

### 12.4 Clinician / Researcher Bridge Statement

> The lived reality of prenatal exposure is integrated, but the systems studying and treating it are often siloed. A child’s sleep, sensory processing, GI comfort, movement, environment, therapy plan, caregiver regulation, and developmental stage can all interact.

## 14. Redesign Prioritization

If time is limited, prioritize in this order:

1. Homepage repositioning
2. Navigation / IA skeleton
3. Sleep page with age lens + troubleshooting + stack model
4. Regulation Stack hub
5. When It’s Not Working hub
6. By Age hub
7. Sensory seeking / sensory avoidance pages
8. Clinicians & Researchers hub
9. Remaining challenge pages
10. Research/source library polish

Why sleep first:

- It is universally recognized as a multiplier.
- It affects both child and parent capacity.
- It clearly demonstrates why age, stack, and troubleshooting matter.
- The lived example of 5am waking and inability to self-soothe gives the site a concrete, relatable anchor.

## 15. Open Design Questions

These should be resolved before implementation or during early prototyping.

1. Should “Early waking” be its own page or a major section inside Sleep?
   - Recommendation: make it a major section inside Sleep first, then split if content grows.

2. Should age filtering be interactive tabs or static sections?
   - Recommendation: static anchor sections first for accessibility, SEO, and simplicity.

3. How prominent should supplements be?
   - Recommendation: include under Nutrition / Physiology with strong evidence/uncertainty labeling and “do not add everything at once” guidance.

4. Should clinician/researcher content be embedded or separate?
   - Recommendation: both. Each parent page gets concise clinician/research notes, while deeper material lives in the Clinicians & Researchers section.

5. How much personal/lived-experience framing should appear?
   - Recommendation: enough to explain why the site exists and validate real problems, but not so much that it feels anecdotal or less credible.

6. Should the stack be visualized as layers or a wheel?
   - Recommendation: use a layered stack for parent clarity; consider a systems map/wheel for clinician/research pages.

## 16. Implementation Notes for Future Redesign Work

When implementing, use this document as the strategic source of truth.

Design should proceed in this sequence:

1. Audit existing content and routes.
2. Build the new navigation skeleton.
3. Build reusable components.
4. Redesign homepage.
5. Create hub pages.
6. Rewrite one flagship challenge page: Sleep.
7. Validate the model with that page.
8. Expand to sensory, meltdowns, feeding/GI, and therapy pages.
9. Build clinician/researcher depth.
10. QA mobile, accessibility, and internal linking.

Do not start by rewriting every page. Start by proving the model on the homepage and Sleep page.

The Sleep page should become the prototype for the entire system:

- age lens
- stack interactions
- troubleshooting
- parent summary
- clinician note
- researcher note
- source handling

Once Sleep works, replicate the pattern.

## 17. Definition of Done for Redesign

The redesign is successful when:

- Parents can quickly find practical guidance without reading academic text first.
- Clinicians can recognize the framework as credible and clinically useful.
- Researchers can see mechanisms, gaps, and cross-domain questions.
- Age differences are built into intervention guidance.
- “When it’s not working” is treated as central, not exceptional.
- The stack model is visible and understandable.
- The site no longer feels like a collection of pages; it feels like a guided system.

Final strategic statement:

> The site should help people move from isolated advice to integrated support: understand the child’s developmental stage, identify the challenge, build the stack around it, and troubleshoot when the first plan fails.
